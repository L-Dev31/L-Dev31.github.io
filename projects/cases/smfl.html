The Brief

While sharing an island with Sint Maarten, the French Collectivité de Saint-Martin lacks an official flag that asserts its distinct cultural identity. Having lived in the Caribbean, I wanted to design a flag that moved beyond standard colonial symbols or generic tricolors.

I designed a comprehensive flag proposal for Saint-Martin. The design is strictly built on the North American Vexillological Association (NAVA) guidelines, validated through 3D wind simulations, and structurally grounded in the island's geography and history.

Designed for the trade winds: recognizable at a distance, structurally stable, and historically grounded.

The Problem

Identity Over Imitation

The challenge was to avoid generic tourist imagery while also rejecting recycled symbols from mainland France. The design needed to honor Saint-Martin’s rich Arawak, Kalinago, and multicultural roots without relying on complex coats of arms or text. Technically, the flag had to remain clean, distinct, and instantly recognizable from a distance, even when draped or flying in heavy winds.

The Process

1. Vexillological Standards

I strictly applied Ted Kaye's Good Flag, Bad Flag principles (NAVA): use a few bold colors, eliminate text or complex heraldry, and ensure the design is simple enough to draw from memory. Every graphical element had to serve a functional purpose.

2. Selecting the Emblem

After testing symbols like the hummingbird and queen conch, I selected the brown pelican. Already part of the Collectivité's official emblem, it offered the strongest silhouette for fabric and serves as a heraldic symbol of protection, maintaining clarity even at scale.

3. Iteration and Wind Simulation

I ran early concepts through 3D flagpole simulators to test real-world physics. While standard horizontal stripes blurred in heavy winds, a saltire (Saint Andrew's cross) paired with a central diamond proved to be the most visually stable structure.

The Design

4. Geographic Color Mapping

The palette acts as a map of the island: azure blue (sky and sea), tropical green (fertile land), and white (referencing Soualiga, the salt pans, and beaches). At the center, black and gold anchor the saltire and shield, honoring the island’s African and Indian heritage.

5. Geometry & Fimbriation

To prevent colors from bleeding together under harsh sunlight, I used a continuous gold outline (fimbriation) to separate the black saltire from the blue and green fields. The central diamond shields and frames the pelican sharply without crowding the icon.

Form dictated by physical constraints and strict vexillological rules.

The Outcome

This project culminated in a complete technical dossier and design rationale submitted to the official Saint-Martin flag initiative. It demonstrates a strict, methodology-first approach to graphic design: marrying cultural history with the physical realities of fabric in motion.
