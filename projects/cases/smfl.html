The Brief

Saint-Martin shares its island with Sint Maarten, and the French side has no official flag of its own. Having lived in the Caribbean, I wanted to design one that goes beyond colonial symbols and generic tricolors.

The result is a complete flag proposal, built on the guidelines of the North American Vexillological Association (NAVA), checked in 3D wind simulations, and grounded in the island's geography and history.

Designed for the trade winds: recognizable at a distance, stable in the wind, rooted in history.

The Problem

Identity Over Imitation

The challenge was to avoid generic tourist imagery and recycled symbols from mainland France. The design had to honor Saint-Martin's Arawak, Kalinago and multicultural roots without a coat of arms or text. It also had to stay clean and recognizable from a distance, even draped or flying in heavy wind.

The Process

1. Vexillological Standards

I applied Ted Kaye's Good Flag, Bad Flag principles (NAVA): a few bold colors, no text or complex heraldry, and a design simple enough to draw from memory. Every element had to have a purpose.

2. Selecting the Emblem

I tested symbols like the hummingbird and the queen conch, then chose the brown pelican. It is already part of the Collectivité's official emblem, it has the strongest silhouette on fabric, and it is a heraldic symbol of protection.

3. Iteration and Wind Simulation

I ran early concepts through 3D flagpole simulators to test real physics. Standard horizontal stripes blurred in heavy wind. A saltire (Saint Andrew's cross) with a central diamond stayed the most readable.

The Design

4. Geographic Color Mapping

The palette works as a map of the island: azure blue for sky and sea, tropical green for fertile land, and white for Soualiga, the salt pans and the beaches. At the center, black and gold anchor the saltire and the shield, honoring the island's African and Indian heritage.

5. Geometry & Fimbriation

To keep the colors from bleeding together in harsh sunlight, a continuous gold outline (fimbriation) separates the black saltire from the blue and green fields. The central diamond frames the pelican without crowding it.

Form set by physical constraints and vexillological rules.

The Outcome

The project ended with a complete technical dossier and design rationale, submitted to the official Saint-Martin flag initiative. The method was the same throughout: cultural history first, then the physics of fabric in motion.
