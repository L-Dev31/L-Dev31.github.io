The Brief

Saint-Martin shares its island with Sint Maarten, but still lacks an official flag asserting its own distinct identity. Having lived in the Caribbean myself, I realized firsthand how French overseas territories lack flags that truly represent their people. They are usually left with either the standard French tricolor or imposed symbols rooted in a colonial past.

Passionate about vexillology, I designed a complete flag proposal for the Collectivité de Saint-Martin: a visual structure built around the guidelines of the North American Vexillological Association, validated through wind simulations, and deeply rooted in Saint-Martin's geography and history.

A flag is not meant to be viewed flat on a screen. It has to withstand the trade winds, read clearly from a distance, and tell its island's story in a single glance.

The Problem

Moving Past Imitation

Designing an identity for a Caribbean territory means avoiding two traps: falling into generic tourist imagery or recycling symbols from mainland France. Saint-Martin, named Soualiga ("the land of salt") by the Arawak and Kalinago peoples, is a crossroads of African, Indian, Caribbean, and European cultures shaped by resilience.

The challenge was both technical and symbolic: create a clean, distinct flag that remains recognizable from far away without relying on complex coats of arms or text, while honoring the island's deep historical roots.

The Process

1. Vexillological Rules

I followed the core principles from Ted Kaye's Good Flag, Bad Flag guide (NAVA). A good design should be simple enough for a child to draw from memory, stick to a few bold colors, eliminate text or complex heraldry, and avoid pure ornament. Every single line has a purpose.

2. Choosing the Symbol

Three symbols were considered. The hummingbird was too delicate and lost all its detail at a distance on fabric. The queen conch felt more like a local resource than a representation of the people. The brown pelican was the clear choice: omnipresent along the coasts, already part of the Collectivité's emblem, and a heraldic symbol of devotion and protection.

3. Iteration and Wind Tests

Every concept was run through a flagpole simulator. Horizontal and diagonal stripe layouts collapsed into an unreadable blur when folded by the wind. A saltire (Saint Andrew's cross) paired with a central diamond proved to be the most visually stable structure, keeping its shape even when rippling in heavy trade winds.

The Design

4. A Color Compass

The flag reads like a map of the island. Azure blue crowns the top for the sky and the surrounding sea, while tropical green anchors the bottom for the fertile land and lush hills. White frames the sides, a nod to Soualiga, the salt pans, the beaches, and peaceful unity. At the heart, black and gold carry heritage and resilience: the saltire and central shield honor African and Indian roots with quiet dignity.

5. Geometry and the Gold Fimbriation

To prevent the black saltire from bleeding into the dark blue or green sections under harsh sunlight, a continuous gold outline (fimbriation) separates the colors. The central diamond keeps sharp edges to form a protective shield around the gold pelican, framing the bird without crowding it.

Every line, color, and angle came from an experiment tested against the wind. This flag keeps only what held up.

What This Built

This project resulted in a technical dossier and rationale submitted as part of the official Saint-Martin flag initiative. It outlines a clear design methodology: combining local history, strict vexillological standards, and the real-world physics of fabric flying in the wind.
