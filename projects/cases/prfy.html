The Brief

In February 2026, in a team of four students at Sophia Ynov Campus, we worked on a UX and product sprint: define a real mobile problem, scope a valid MVP with structured product methods, and prototype four individual solutions in Figma. The constraint was deliberate: one concept, four design interpretations, each graded on UX rigor rather than aesthetic preference.

Léo Tosku (UI design) · Benoîte Macchi (UX research) · Ryan Aversa (UX research) · Margaux Halby (UX research)

Every time I needed a warranty, the receipt was gone. That is a UX gap.

The Problem

Physical Receipts in a Digital World

Warranties exist on paper, and paper gets lost. When you need proof of purchase for a return or a broken product under guarantee, the receipt is never where you left it. Existing solutions require an account (and hand your purchase data to a third party), rely on email forwarding, or are too generic. The opportunity: a zero-account, scan-first mobile vault for receipts and warranties, legally usable, stored locally and always accessible.

The Process

1. Scoping the MVP

We used MoSCoW prioritization to cut down. Must-haves: OCR receipt scanning, manual warranty entry, a browsable store list, instant search, and a detail page per receipt with PDF export. Deferred: dark mode, discount coupon management, email import and QR codes at checkout. The goal was a focused core that could ship.

2. User Stories

Five user stories anchored every interface decision: the hurried user who needs to scan a receipt in under ten seconds, the budget tracker browsing spending by store and month, the privacy-conscious buyer who refuses to create an account, the warranty hunter who wants a notification before a product expires, and the searcher who needs to find any receipt instantly. Every screen traces back to one of them.

As a user in a hurry, I want to scan my receipt with my camera to save my purchase instantly, without retyping anything.
The Scanner · Speed & capture
As someone watching their budget, I want to browse my receipts by store and date to know how much I spent this month.
The Tracker · History & clarity
As a privacy-conscious user, I want my receipts to stay on my phone only, with no online account required.
The Private User · Data ownership
As an electronics buyer, I want a notification before my warranty expires so I can act before it’s too late.
The Warranty Hunter · Peace of mind
As a frequent shopper, I want to find any receipt instantly by store name or amount without scrolling.
The Searcher · Instant retrieval

3. Architecture & User Flow

The app has five surfaces: a home screen with store cards, a per-store receipt list sorted by date, a receipt detail page with PDF download, a central scan button in the navigation that opens the camera directly, and a verification screen after OCR where the user can correct the detected data before saving. Settings covers storage, notifications and theme. There is no account screen, by design.

4. Figma Prototype: Light Glassmorphic UI

My individual prototype in Figma used a light glassmorphic visual language: white and soft blue surfaces with frosted glass depth, very dark navy for primary text and light grey for secondary labels. The target was a native iPhone feel: familiar, quick to use, premium without being cold. The scan flow resolves in three taps: open the camera, confirm the scan, save. The detail page shows only what matters: store name, date, total amount and guarantee expiry.

The scan button lives in the navigation because the product only works if adding a receipt takes less time than losing one.

The Outcome

A complete product definition exercise: problem framing, MoSCoW scoping, user stories, information architecture, user flow mapping and a Figma prototype, with four individual interpretations of the same brief in one team. It built a base in structured product thinking: define the scope before touching a frame, tie every interface decision to a named user and a specific friction, and know what to cut before knowing what to build.
